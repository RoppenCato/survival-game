# The blót as a rite: what the sources say, and how we stage it

Colin, AD, 2026-10-29. Robin: "You need to do some research into Blot and sacrifice so that there is some ritual to it, and not just
people standing around in a circle doing the sacrifice at the altar animation." This is the research, then the village's blót as
beats, then the art it needs. The cut-scene tool (`docs/cutscenes.md`) stages it.

## What the sources say

**Snorri's account** (Heimskringla, the saga of Hakon the Good, chapter 16, "About sacrifices") is the fullest description we have.
In Laing's translation:
- The farmers came to the temple and brought everything they needed for the feast, and every man brought ale.
- "All kinds of cattle, as well as horses, were slaughtered." The blood was called *hlaut* and caught in *hlaut*-bowls.
- With *hlaut*-staves, "like sprinkling brushes", the altars and the temple walls, inside and out, were sprinkled with the blood,
  and so were the people.
- The flesh was boiled for those present. The fire was in the middle of the floor with the kettles over it.
- The full goblets were handed across the fire. The chief who held the feast blessed the goblets and all the meat.
- Then came the toasts in their order:
  - first Odin's, for victory and power to the king
  - then Njord's and Frey's, "for a good year and peace" (*til árs ok friðar*)
  - then for many the *bragafull*, the chieftain's cup, over which vows were made
  - last the *minni*, a cup to the memory of departed kin
- At a Yule feast at Mære the farmers made the Christian king eat bits of horse liver and drink the cups without making the sign of
  the cross over them. To share the meat and the cup was to take part.

**Other sources and finds:**
- **Adam of Bremen** (about 1070) on Uppsala: every ninth year, nine males of every kind were offered, and the bodies were hung in
  the grove beside the temple. The gods were called on by need: Thor for plague and famine, Odin for war, Frey for marriages.
- **The Icelandic law texts** speak of an arm ring that the chieftain (the *goði*) reddened with the blood of the beasts he
  offered. Oaths were sworn on it, and a ring lay on the altar of each main temple.
- **The feast:** after the offering, everyone ate and drank together. That shared meal is the best attested part of the rite.
- **The seasons:**
  - the winter nights' blót, for a good year
  - midwinter (Yule)
  - midsummer at Mære
  - the victory blót at summer's start
- **The finds, which I give as notes:**
  - At Frösö in Jämtland, bones of bear, elk, pig and other beasts lay round the stump of a birch under the church: a tree where
    offerings were hung.
  - At Hofstaðir in Iceland, cattle skulls were set up on the hall's outside wall.
  - The rite was held at a hall or a *vé* (a hallowed place), not only at a stone.
- **Hallowing a place with fire:** Landnámabók tells of settlers carrying fire round the land they took, to make it theirs. It is a
  rite we can use to hallow the ground before the offering.

**Cautions:**
- Snorri was a Christian writing two centuries after the conversion, and some of his words (*hlautbolli*, *hlautteinn*) may be his.
  Even so, his order is the best we have.
- Modern heathen guides add steps (cords round the place, set prayers) that are not in the old texts. I have left them out.
- A wolf was not a beast of the blót: the offerings were horses, cattle, swine, goats, the beasts of the farm. In our story the
  village takes the wolf as the season's due. I suggest it is offered with the farm beast it threatened (below, for Robin to
  decide).

## The village's blót, as beats

The rite's backbone, from Snorri: **gather, hallow, offer, redden, boil, bless, the fulls, the memory**. Then our turn, the gods'
answer: the bolt, and the runes. Each beat is something the eye can read from above, done by many people, not by one stab.

1. **The gathering at dusk.** The folk come in a file from the houses, each carrying something: ale in a bucket or tub, bread, a
   basket. They set it down by the hearth and take their places on two sides of the *vé*. Children run ahead, an old man is helped
   along. (carry_walk, then standing)
2. **The hallowing.** The *goði* lights a torch from the great fire and walks once round the altar, the hearth and the people
   carrying the fire, sunwise (east, then south, then west). The crowd turns to follow the flame. (a torch walk)
3. **The beasts led in.** The hero brings the wolf's carcass to the stone. Two men lead the farm's offering, a young bull or a
   ram, garlanded, to the stone. The crowd falls quiet. (walk; the beast led)
4. **The ring.** The *goði* lifts the oath ring from the altar, holds it up to the people and puts it on his arm.
5. **The offering.** The blow, quick and done by the *goði*, not the hero: the beast falls. A man catches the blood in the *hlaut*
   bowl. (the sacrifice clip on the godi; the beast's death; kneel with the bowl)
6. **The reddening**, the most striking part of the rite and the one to linger on.
   - The *goði* dips the twig bundle and sprinkles the altar stone, then the posts of the *vé*.
   - Then he walks down the line of people, flicking blood over them. Each bows their head as he passes.
   - The hero is reddened last: the stranger taken into the village's rite.
   - The ring is reddened too.
7. **The kettle.** The meat goes into the great kettle over the fire. Steam rises, and the people sit down on both sides of the
   fire. (sit; the kettle's steam)
8. **The blessing and the fulls.** The *goði* holds the horn over the fire, hallows it, and passes it across the flames.
   - **Odin's full**, for victory: the men raise their cups.
   - **Njord's and Frey's full**, *til árs ok friðar*, for a good year and peace: everyone raises them.
   - **The memory.** The horn is lifted in silence for the dead. The *goði* pours a little on the ground, and the hero's thoughts
     can go to the lost crew.
9. **The answer** (the game's turn). The hero is offered the horn last. As he drinks:
   - the sky darkens, thunder comes, and the bolt strikes the stone
   - the stones rise
   - the motes flow to the hero, and the runes come to him (the scripted first pick)
10. **The feast, and the teaching.** The people eat and the noise comes back. The *goði* comes to the hero and tells him to raise
    his own stone and bring the autumn's offering there (the altar taught). The scene ends with the feast going on as play
    resumes.

Pacing: about 70 to 90 seconds with every beat. The skip lands at the feast. The book's Quests page keeps what the *goði* said.
Beats 1, 7 and 10 can play out as ambient life while the player watches, without holding the camera.

## The art it needs (the studio's)

**People's clips:**
- `sprinkle`: dip the twigs in the bowl held low, then flick them out in an arc
- `raise_cup`: a horn or cup lifted high and held, then lowered
- `drink`: drink from a horn
- `hail`: both arms raised, the crowd's answer to a toast
- `bow`: head and shoulders bowed, standing
- `pour`: tip the horn to the ground
- `walk_torch`: a walk holding a torch up

**Hand things for people:** the torch, the *hlaut* bowl, the twig bundle, a drinking horn, the oath ring.

**Set pieces:**
- **the *vé***: four carved posts round the altar, a hearth, a tree hung with skulls and offerings (Frösö, Uppsala), and cattle
  skulls on the posts (Hofstaðir)
- **the great kettle** on a tripod over the fire, with a steam loop
- **the ale tubs and baskets** the folk bring
- **the altar reddened**, as a second picture

**The beast led:** a garlanded ram or young bull from the farm animals, with a halter rope.

**Already there:** kneel, sit, carry_walk, interact and pickup for the people; sacrifice, drink and war_cry for the hero; the altar,
the blood decals, the bolt, the rising stones and the motes.

**What is Robin's to decide:**
- whether the farm beast is offered with the wolf
- whether the *goði* or the hero strikes the blow (I suggest the *goði*: the hero's turn comes with the horn and the gods' answer)
- the season (the chapter has it at midsummer's dusk, which matches Mære's midsummer blót)

# Idea bank

Ideas for the game that are not decided yet. Things Robin has decided live in `CLAUDE.md` ("The game design, as
decided so far"); when an idea here is decided, move it there and delete it here.

Each entry says who it came from. "Claude" entries are suggestions, not decisions.

## Open questions (Robin, 2026-10-04)

These need an answer before the systems around them can be built.

### 1. How do places come back in a natural way?

Robin: unclaimed villages, castles and other places are repopulated or rebuilt after winter, in generated ways.
Every year should have some sort of end, so it is obvious that time passes.

Suggestions (Claude):
- **Winter is the year's end.** The sea freezes or gets too rough to sail, so everyone is home. A short "the year
  turns" moment (a feast at your hall, a summary of the year: raids, losses, new members, claims) and then spring.
  The world changes while nobody can see it, which hides the regeneration.
- **Spring news.** When the ice breaks, traders and travellers bring word of what changed: "a new lord holds the
  castle at ...", "the monks have returned to ...". The player learns the new map by hearing about it.
- **Places remember.** A village raided last year comes back poorer but with a palisade; one raided three years
  running is abandoned, or gets a garrison. One left alone grows richer. This makes regeneration a consequence,
  not a reset.
- **Ruins first.** A burnt place shows as a ruin for the rest of that year, with settlers rebuilding from autumn.

### 2. How do you recruit members?

Robin: probably several different ways.

Suggestions (Claude):
- Captives taken in raids who choose to join, or thralls you free.
- Sellswords and landless men hired at trading villages and the hub (cost silver, loyal while paid).
- Volunteers who come to you as your name grows, each spring.
- People you rescue or help (a shipwrecked crew, a farmer's son after you clear wolves).
- Rivals' men who switch sides when you beat their leader.
- Children of your own village growing up over the years (slow, but free and loyal).
- Each way could give a different kind of member: fighters, rowers, craftsmen, farmers.

### 3. Legendary Vikings with big ships and big bases

Robin: their names need to spread through the world somehow, so you know about them.

Suggestions (Claude):
- **Skalds and gossip.** Traders, priests and your own members talk about them ("Arnor: they say Ragnhild Red-Sail
  took three ships to the south coast"). Lines change with what the legend did that year.
- **Runestones.** Stones raised where they won a battle or lost a friend, readable in the world.
- **What they leave behind.** A village already burnt when you arrive, with their mark on the gate.
- **Seen at sea.** A big striped sail on the horizon that you can follow or flee.
- **The spring news** (see 1) names them and where they wintered.
- **Your own name** spreads the same way, so you can hear people talk about you.
- They could be beaten, befriended, or served for a season: one of the "several ways to progress".

### 4. Attacks on your village and claims, without annoying random raids

Robin: wants some type of attack on your villages and claimed territory, but dislikes random raids as in other
survival games. They are annoying and you have to repair your base afterwards.

Suggestions (Claude):
- **Attacks are answers, never random.** Raid a lord's lands and he may come for you; leave people alone and
  nobody comes. The player always knows why.
- **Warning first.** A messenger, a beacon, or word from traders a few days ahead, with the attacker's strength.
  You can prepare, pay them off, meet them at sea, or strike first.
- **Fights happen at the edge.** Attackers land at the beach or the palisade, and the fight is for a marker
  (the hall door, the claim's banner), not for smashing every building.
- **Losing costs goods, not hours of rebuilding.** They carry off silver, stores or livestock, or take the claim.
  Buildings are scorched and repaired by your members over time, not piece by piece by the player.
- **Claims far from home are the weak ones.** Outposts get tested; the home village rarely does. Members left as
  a garrison decide the outcome, so it is a decision about where to put people.
- **Only in the sailing season.** Nobody attacks in winter, so winter is always safe time to build.

### 5. Additions to the map layout

The layout itself is decided (see `CLAUDE.md`): the compass means something, the details are generated. Two
additions were suggested with it and are not decided (Claude):
- **Named lands, shuffled.** The world is built from a handful of lands, each generated in its own style (a land
  of monasteries, a coast of walled towns, an ice land, a river land). Which ones appear changes per world; the
  compass rule decides roughly where. You hear of them before you find them.
- **A few fixed landmarks.** A small number of set pieces, such as the trading hub, exist in every world.
- What exactly lies in each direction (which biomes, which kinds of places) still has to be worked out.

### 6. Places from the Viking age that could be in the game (Claude)

To raid:
- Island and coastal monasteries (Lindisfarne and Iona were the famous first targets), Irish monasteries with
  round towers, where monks hid with their treasure.
- River towns far inland, reached by rowing up a river (Paris on the Seine, Dorestad on the Rhine).
- Walled towns and forts built as an answer to raids (the Anglo-Saxon burhs), fortified bridges that block a river.

To trade:
- Hedeby, Birka and Kaupang at home; Dublin and Jorvik (York), which Vikings founded or took over.
- Gotland, an island of traders known for buried silver hoards and picture stones.
- The eastern river road: Staraya Ladoga, Holmgard (Novgorod), Kiev, and at the far end Miklagard
  (Constantinople), where Northmen served as the emperor's guard. Ships were dragged overland between rivers.
- The far north: trade with the Sami for furs and with hunters for walrus ivory.

To explore and settle:
- The Faroes, Shetland and Orkney as stepping stones; Iceland (volcanoes, hot springs, no king); Greenland;
  and Vinland at the edge of the world.
- Lofoten and the northern fishing grounds; the Moskstraumen maelstrom.

At home and in your own lands:
- Thing sites (where free people meet to settle disputes), ring fortresses (Trelleborg), ship burials and great
  mounds, royal runestones (Jelling), the Danevirke wall, temples and sacred groves (Uppsala).
- Winter camps and ship forts on foreign shores (the longphorts), which is how a raid became a claim.

Natural places: fjords, glaciers, sea ice, bird cliffs, whale and walrus grounds, hot springs.

## Gathering: making chopping, mining and picking satisfying (Robin, 2026-10-07; ideas, not decided)

Robin's brief: more satisfying and interesting, never bothersome or complicated. References: Valheim's falling trees that
fell other trees; Dragonwilds' magic that fells many; Palworld's hit points on a thing.

**Built in the Gathering Editor (2026-10-07, each a switch, none decided yet):** the sweet spot (a glint on one side of a
tree or one facet of a rock that wanders; a blow from there bites deeper), rhythm (a blow in the window after the last is
clean; mashing misses it), the fall (a tree falls away from you, fells the trees it lands on, hurts you if you stand
there), trunks (a felled tree lies as a trunk and gives its wood a log at a time), nests (eggs, or honey and angry
bees), stumps growing back after some days, cracks on rocks, and toughness sliders. Robin tries them and hands over the
settings with "Use in the game".

**Not built, for later:** a special stroke (a heavy blow that splits a trunk at once), the knife's cutting of herbs and
reeds with a sweet spot of its own, quarrying big stone in blocks, a thing's hit points shown by its look alone (Palworld),
a log that rolls downhill, tapping birch sap in spring, rings in a stump that tell its age.

## Copper, charcoal and the cave (Robin decided 2026-10-07; being built)

**Decided:** charcoal comes from **morning embers** (a few lumps at any fire that burned through a night), the **charcoal clamp**
(a mound of logs under turf, lit, smoking for a day, a column of smoke over the trees; tending it when the smoke thins yields
more, leaving it still yields), and **lightning-struck trees and old fire rings** found in the wild. **The furnace is plain:**
load ore and charcoal, wait, take the bar; copper tools and weapons are workbench recipes from bars. **Copper** stays rare in
loose rocks; the real source is **green veins on the cave walls**, mined out for good (an island's cave is a hoard you spend).
**The troll's hoard** in his lair is open on the nights he walks out; outside he **walks about near the mouth where you can
see him and sneak past**, so being inside is tense: morning brings him back. **The cave is rebuilt as a smooth field** (no
square walls): winding passages, pockets, stalactites, rubble, water. **All of this is built (2026-10-07); see `CLAUDE.md`.**

**Not chosen (kept for later):** the burnt hall's charred logs as charcoal; a charcoal burner living at a clamp (a person who
could join); peat from a bog; bellows pumped in rhythm (Fantasy Life's blacksmith) and stone moulds that choose the tool;
old workings in the cave (shoring, a ruined furnace at the mouth, a miner's pack); rubble walls opened with the pick; a rope
drop to a lower level; petrified older trolls as ore; nuggets in a cave stream.

## A pet (Robin, 2026-10-23)

Robin's words: "Having a pet (dog, wolf, cat etc). The pet would follow next to you, and idle around your base when at home.
The pet should have some sort of control system. For example holding a button, let's say Control, would change your keys to
pet commands. Like Ctrl + mouse click 1 is attack. You would get a yellow circle around the mouse and mousing over things you
can interact with a pet on would highlight it and say what it does. For example 'Attack' when highlighting an enemy."

Notes toward it (Claude, not decided):
- **The dog fits the age** (the Vikings kept dogs, hunting and herding; the elkhound and the spitz types; a wolf would be a
  found-as-a-pup story, a cat a trader's rarity): the first pet a dog, met or bought, named by the player as the hero is.
- **Where it lives in the code:** the hird's follow and home routines are most of it (a hirdman follows, works and idles at
  home; the pet follows anywhere, as a hirdman does with no stone raised, and idles about the hearth at home); a beast's body
  from the engine (a wolf's speed, a bite) on your side (`e.side` 1), so it fights as a friend does, goes down instead of
  dying, and gets up. The animals' sheets already carry the wolf; a dog needs Colin, AD's model.
- **The command mode:** Ctrl held (the key is free in the game only while no foe is targeted: Ctrl targets the nearest foe
  today, so the pet's key may want to be another, or Ctrl held longer) shows the yellow ring at the mouse and a word over
  whatever it points at: "Attack" on a foe, "Fetch" on a drop, "Flush" on a bush with a hidden beast, "Stay", "Come", "Guard
  here" on the ground, "Track" on a blood trail (the deer's trail is there already); a click gives the order. The ring and
  the word are the interface's and mine; the pet's own lines (barks) are nobody's.
- **What a pet is for in play:** the hunt (it finds and flushes deer and boar, tracks a bleeding deer, holds a boar so you
  can strike), the watch at night (it wakes you to the wolves, barks at a stranger at your door), a carrier of small things,
  and company on the long walks. Never a meter to feed; a fed dog is quicker, an unfed one slower, as the hird.
- **In the lore:** what folk believe about dogs and wolves is Alistair, LD's to say (the hound at the door, the dog in the
  grave with its master).

## Weather and biomes: reward, not punishment (Claude, revised 2026-10-04)

The rules are decided (see `CLAUDE.md`, design conventions): no meters that drain and hurt you, nothing that cuts
stamina, no wetness. Being unprepared is simply the normal state; doing the right thing adds a buff on top. Robin
likes a rested or comfort buff. The examples below are not decided. An earlier version made the cold slow your
stamina; that is dropped.

| Where | Unprepared | Doing right | Reward | Why it is worth having |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Home, any season | Normal | Sleep or sit in a warm, furnished house | "Rested": lasts a long time, longer the more comfortable the home (fire, bed, furs, bathhouse) | A reason to build a nice home and not only walls |
| Cold biome | Normal | Fur clothing | "Warm": extra health while in the cold | The animals there hit harder, so the extra health is what lets you fight them. Gives hunting and tailoring a purpose |
| Cold biome | Normal | Warm up at a fire | "Thawed": health comes back faster for some minutes after you leave | Camps become stepping stones, so you plan a route |
| Cold biome | Normal | Hot meal or warm drink | Makes the warmth buffs last longer | Ties cooking to exploring |
| Deep snow | Slower walking | Skis or snowshoes | Faster on snow than on grass | Skis are Viking-age. The obstacle becomes the fast road |
| Frozen lakes and fjords | Normal | Bone skates | Very fast travel on ice | Winter opens routes that summer closes |
| Hot southern lands | Normal | Light linen clothing, carry water | "Cool": extra health, or a longer Rested buff, while in the heat | The south needs different gear from the north |
| Rain | Normal, and nothing gets "wet" | Raid in the rain | Guards hear you later | Weather becomes a chance to take |
| Fog | Hard to see far | Approach a target in fog | Guards see you much later | Same |
| Storm at sea | Slower, pushed off course | Reef the sail, full crew at the oars | Ride the storm wind at great speed | Risky and exciting, never a shipwreck out of nowhere |
| Wind | Rowing speed | Sail with the wind, wait for a good day | Much faster crossing | Reading the weather becomes a skill |
| Night | Hard to see | Go without a torch | Harder to be seen | A choice between seeing and being seen |
| Winter at home | Normal | A full larder and a feast | The clan starts spring with a morale buff | The year's work pays off, and it marks the year's end |
| Volcanic land | Normal | Bathe in a hot spring | Strong healing for a while | A reason to sail far |

How the difficulty then works: each biome's animals and enemies are tuned for a prepared player. The weather
itself does nothing to you. Without the buffs you have less health than the place expects, exactly like going
without food in Valheim.

### Midgardsormen, the World Serpent (Robin, 2026-10-14: huge and mythological, appearing and moving the sea; a suggestion, nothing decided)

**The sources, looked up on 2026-10-14.** In the Eddas he is Jörmungandr, Old Norse *Miðgarðsormr*, the Midgard Serpent:
the middle child of Loki and the giantess Angrboða, whom Odin threw into the sea round Midgard, where he grew until he lay
round the whole world biting his own tail (Snorri's *Gylfaginning*). Three stories: at Útgarða-Loki's hall Thor is set to
lift a grey cat and gets one paw off the floor; the cat is the serpent, and the giant says the whole hall was afraid when
the serpent's back touched the sky. Thor fishes with the giant Hymir, baits his hook with the head of Hymir's biggest ox,
rows further out than Hymir dares, and the serpent takes it; Thor hauls so hard his feet go through the boat's bottom to
the sea floor, the serpent's head comes up blowing venom, Thor glares at it and lifts the hammer, and Hymir in terror cuts
the line (*Hymiskviða*, *Gylfaginning*, *Húsdrápa*; the picture stones of Altuna, Ardre and Hørdum, and the Gosforth slab,
show the fishing, Altuna with the feet through the boat). At Ormrök he lets go of his tail, **the sea surges over the
land**, he comes ashore beside the wolf spraying venom over sky and sea, Thor kills him and walks nine steps before the
venom kills Thor (*Völuspá*, *Gylfaginning*). The later Norwegian sea serpent, the *sjøorm*, is the same creature come
down in folk belief: Erik Valkendorf's letter to the Pope (1520), Olaus Magnus (1555: a serpent of the rifts and caves off
Bergen, two hundred feet long, black scales, flaming red eyes, a mane of hair down the neck, out on calm summer nights to
take cattle and lift men off the deck), Peder Claussøn Friis (a head as big as a *jekt*, a body as thick as a barrel),
Hans Egede off Greenland (1734: three or four times his ship, blowing like a whale), Pontoppidan. Seen only in **calm, fair
weather**, hiding in sea caves between. Nothing in any source gives him a colour or a face beyond that; the ouroboros,
the venom, the mane and the red eyes are the whole of the tradition, and the rest is ours.

**The suggestion.** Not a monster to fight: **the one thing in the world that is bigger than the world**. He is the sea's
own weather. What the player ever sees is a part of him, never the whole, and he is why the ocean is dangerous.

- **Found:** the deep sea, far from any coast, and more the further west (the open ocean and the unknown; the compass
  rule). Never in a strait, never in sight of home. In a sea cave now and then: the *sjøorm*'s rifts and caves off the
  coast are where a coil lies sleeping, and the sea cave of the mountains could one day hold one.
- **Lore (the card):** Loki's child, thrown into the sea by Odin, grown round the whole world with his tail in his mouth.
  Thor had him on a hook once and the giant cut the line. When he lets go of his tail the sea comes over the land and the
  world ends. The fishermen of the west say he sleeps in the deep and turns in his sleep, and that the long swell on a
  windless day is his breathing.
- **How he shows:** in stages, each rarer than the last, and never where you can get at him.
  1. **The swell.** On a calm day far out, the sea begins to move: long slow waves from no wind, the boat lifted and set
     down, the wake pushed aside. The ambient sound gets a slow boom and the hull creaks. Nothing is seen. This is the one
     the player meets first and oftenest, and most never learn what it was.
  2. **The coil.** A black, glistening ridge rises out of the water a few hundred units off, the length of several
     longships, scaled, with weed and barnacles on it like the sea cave's walls, and sinks again; the water runs off it in
     sheets, and the wave it makes rolls under the boat. A second rises further off, then a third: the coils of one body
     passing beneath you. Gulls scatter. Nothing can be done to it; an arrow sticks in it as in a hill.
  3. **The head.** Once in a long while, at dusk or in fog, the head: a mane of hair streaming water, two red eyes the
     size of shields, the jaw, and the breath that hangs on the water like steam and sickens whoever rows through it (the
     venom: health draining slowly while you are in the mist, the reward-not-punish rule kept by making it a thing you
     can row out of). He looks at the boat, and sinks. The water he leaves behind is poison for a while: fish float up
     dead, and a boat that stays in it loses rowers to sickness.
  4. **The wake.** A wave that is wrong: a single long roller crossing the sea at an angle to every other, with nothing
     behind it, that lifts the boat and rolls on to break on a coast miles away. That is him moving in the deep. A village
     on that shore has a story about it afterwards (the living world: word spreads).
- **What he does to the sea, in play:** the only true hazard at sea that is not weather. A coil rising under the boat
  throws it (the hull lifted, the crew flung from the thwarts, cargo loose: a chest of silver can go over the side), and
  the raft is simply swamped. The swell makes rowing slow and the sail useless for a while. The venom mist is a sickness
  with a cure at home. **He never chases and never hunts**: he is not after you, you are in his way. So he gives the deep
  sea its fear without a chase mechanic, and the near seas stay safe: **the further from the coasts you dare, the more of
  him you meet**, which fits the compass (the west is the unknown) and the scale problem (the sea is the huge thing).
- **What he gives:** the fishermen's lore and a page in the book, earned by *seeing* him, not by a kill (the book could
  grow by sightings: the silhouette of the swell first, the coil, then the head). A **scale** shed where a coil surfaced,
  floating, black and as big as a shield: the one drop, rare, a thing of legend to carry home (a shield of it that no
  arrow pierces; or the proof that makes a legendary Viking's name). The fisherman's hut and the old men of the shore
  places have lines about him once you have seen the swell, and Arnor has one.
- **Thor's fishing as the far goal (an idea, not a mechanic):** the only way to "fight" him is Thor's: a hook forged for
  it, an ox's head, the longship rowed further out than anyone goes, and the strength to hold the line; the myth ends with
  the line cut, and so could ours (a legendary Viking who held him for a minute). Something for the far west, years in.
- **Weakness, as a card would put it:** none. The card says only *where he is not*: never in sight of land, never in a
  storm, never under a ship with the gods' luck (a dragon stem, a sacrifice before sailing, a rune on the hull: the
  reward side, things that keep him down).

**How it could be built (so it stays cheap):** he is **never one creature in the engine**. The swell is the water's own
drawing plus the vessel's physics (a long wave field added under the boat; the Sea Editor's handling takes it as a push).
A coil is a prop on the water: one Blender set piece, a scaled ridge rendered like the sea cave's mouth, drawn with foam
and run-off, animated by rising and sinking (two or three frames or a scale curve), placed by a `serpentTick` that chooses
a line beneath the boat and raises coils along it in turn. The head is a second set piece with its own rise and sink and
the mist as a particle field that lingers. The wake is a travelling wave in the water field. None of it needs collision
beyond the boat's hull being lifted. So: a water field that things can push, two set pieces, a mist, a tick that
decides when (calm weather, far from land, the deep, rarer near home) and the lines and the page. The sea's own weather
(wind, fog, swell) would want to exist first, which the time and season work will bring; he is the reason to build it.

**The art concept (2026-10-14, what I imagine; it is built for the gameplay above, so each picture is one of the four
stages and nothing else).** The rule that governs it all: **the screen never holds him**. Every picture is a piece of
him crossing the edge of the view, drawn so the eye finishes the body off-screen. That is how a 2D top-down game, which
shows 29 by 18 metres, can carry a creature that goes round the world: not by drawing him big, but by never drawing an
end to him.

- **The body.** A sea-black coil with a bottle-green sheen where the light catches it, in the ink look like everything
  else: a heavy brown-black line on the shadow side, grain in the fill, one cool shadow. Scales the size of shields,
  drawn as overlapping plates with a lit edge each, as the wall slabs of the mountains are drawn, so he reads as rock
  that moves. Weed hangs off him and barnacles sit in the hollows between the plates, in the sea cave's palette, so he
  and the sea cave belong to each other and the player who has been in the cave recognises the surface. The scale of a
  coil is set by the longship: **a coil rising is at least twice as long as the longship and as high as its mast**, and the
  longship is already longer than the screen. Three or four frames of rising and sinking, the water running off him in
  sheets and foam boiling along his length, as one Blender set piece rendered from the game's camera like the cave
  mouths, and a mirrored one so two coils in a row are not the same.
- **The head.** Only ever half out of the water, the chin on the surface, the rest hidden by the swell and the mist. A
  long reptile skull like a longship's prow seen from above, which is on purpose: the dragon stem on the player's own ship
  is the shape men made of him, and the first time a player sees the head they should feel that the stem was a copy. A
  **mane** from the crown down the neck, the sea serpent's hair of the old accounts, drawn as the people's hair is drawn
  but streaming and heavy with water, with the water pouring out of it. **Two red eyes**, the one hot colour of the
  picture, set far apart and glowing as the wolves' eyes glow at night, so they are the thing seen first through fog, and
  they can be drawn alone when the rest is hidden (two red points on the water far off, which is the cheapest and
  scariest picture of all). No teeth shown: the jaw stays shut on the tail he is not biting any more, and the venom comes
  as **breath**, a pale green-grey steam that lies on the water and drifts, in the mist technique of the lighting trials.
  His gaze is the one animation that matters: the head turns, the eyes find the boat and hold it, then the whole thing
  sinks without hurry.
- **What the water does.** Most of the picture is water, so most of the art is the sea's own drawing changed: the long
  swell as a field under the waves that lifts the boat and tilts it (the hull's own drawing already turns with the
  heading; a tilt is a small shear), a ring of foam where a coil breaks the surface, a slick of flattened water behind a
  coil that has sunk with bubbles rising in it, the dead fish floating belly-up in the poisoned water, and the wake: one
  dark line across the sea, longer than the screen, with a white crest, moving. At dusk the swell takes the sky's colour
  and the coil is a black cut-out against it; by night only the eyes, and the sound.
- **Sound before sight.** Every stage has a sound first: a slow boom under the hull like the sea cave's, the hull's
  creak, gulls leaving, the hiss of water off the scales, the breath. The ambient sound already has beds and single
  sounds; he would be a bed that comes up for a minute and goes.
- **The book's page.** The only place he is drawn whole: an old chart's picture, the ouroboros round a small drawn world
  with the islands you know on it, in the ink of the runestones, with the four stages as four small vignettes down the
  margin that fill in as they are seen (the swell as a line of waves, a coil, the head, the wake), and Olaus Magnus's
  serpent round a ship as the last, which is Thor's fishing and is never filled.
- **What he must not be:** a dragon (no wings, no legs, no fire; a worm), cartoon-cute (no grin, no round eyes; the eyes
  are slits under a heavy brow, as the trolls' are), a boss with a health bar, or ever seen whole on the screen.

**Open:** whether he can be met in the home waters at all (I say never: the two islands are too near); whether the coil
should be able to wreck a boat outright or only throw what is loose (I say throw: dying at sea is meant to be forgiving);
whether the head should ever be drawn in full or only ever half out of the water (half: the whole is for the book's
page); and whether the sea cave's dry pocket is where a sleeping coil lies, so the sea cave on a real mountain island is
where he is first seen close.

## Perks and levelling (Robin's direction, 2026-10-05; not decided)

Robin wants a perk system: you level up and choose between attacking skills from fighting, and choose skills
from the other things you do (chopping, mining, cooking, running). In the end you can get everything, but you
choose from the start.

### Options (Claude)

- **A. Skills that grow by doing, no choices** (Valheim): swing an axe and your axe skill rises; it only gives
  numbers. Fits "from the things you do" but there is nothing to choose.
- **B. One pool of points** (classic RPG): any activity gives experience, you spend points anywhere. Choices, but
  the link between what you did and what you get is gone.
- **C. A craft per activity, each with its own small tree of runes** *(recommended)*. Every activity is a
  **craft** that levels by doing it: Sword, Bow, Shield, Axe (chopping), Pickaxe (mining), Cooking, Running,
  Building, later Sailing and Rowing. Each craft has three tiers of two or three **runes** (the perks). At craft
  levels 5, 10 and 15 you pick one rune of that tier; every five levels after that you may take one you passed
  over. So you choose first, and in the end you hold them all, and what you did is what you got better at.

### What the runes could be (C)

| Craft | Tier 1 (level 5) | Tier 2 (level 10) | Tier 3 (level 15) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sword | Follow-through: the third blow sweeps wider / Riposte: a parry opens a free heavy blow | Shield wall: block costs less stamina / Berserk: more damage below half health | **Whirlwind** (active, hotbar): a spin that hits all round |
| Bow | Steady hand: arrows fly straighter / Quick draw: faster shots | Hunter's eye: more damage to animals / Fletcher: arrows are cheaper to make | **Pinning shot** (active): the target cannot move for a moment |
| Axe | Clean cut: trees need one chop less / Splinters: a chance of extra wood | Lumberjack: chopping costs no stamina / Timber!: a falling tree hurts what it lands on | Woodwise: oak and the far island's trees give rare wood |
| Pickaxe | Hard swing: rocks break sooner / Vein sense: ore shows on the chart | Stonemason: stone pieces cost less | Deep delver: a chance of flint and later ore from any rock |
| Cooking | Hearty: meals last longer / Second helping: cook two for the price of one | Feast: a meal shared raises the whole hird | Chef: a new dish at the hearth |
| Running | Long wind: stamina returns faster / Sure feet: sprint is faster | Wanderer: the chart reveals more as you walk | Sprinter: a dash costs less |
| Building | Thrifty: pieces cost less / Sturdy: pieces take more damage | Roofwright: bigger rooms get a roof | Hall: the longhouse pieces |

Active runes (tier 3 of the fighting crafts) are what fill the fight hotbar's slots 1 to 6 (decided earlier: skills on

### Robin's shape (2026-10-05): runes you find and slot

Robin: a set number of skill slots, by kind (attacking, utility, mobility, and so on). Skills are **runes** you earn,
find or get from various sources, and you drag the rune you want into a slot to use it. An interface with slots.

Expanded (Claude):

**Runes are things.** A rune is an item: a small carved stone with a skill on it. It sits in the bag like an axe
does, can be dropped, looted, traded, and given by people. That makes skills part of raiding, exploring and
trading at once, which is the game's point. The Item Editor's sibling, a Rune Editor, would make them.

**Slots by kind.** The hero has a rune board with a fixed number of slots, each of a kind:
- **Attack** (2 slots): active skills used from the fight hotbar: Whirlwind, Pinning shot, Shield bash, Berserk.
- **Utility** (2): mostly passive, some active: Hearty (meals last longer), Fletcher (cheap arrows), Clean cut,
  Second helping, Wanderer (the chart fills in as you walk), Night eyes.
- **Mobility** (1): Sure feet (faster sprint), Long wind (stamina back faster), Sprinter (cheap dash), Sea legs.
- **Guard** (1): Shield wall, Riposte, Thick skin.
- **Hird** (1, later): runes that act on your followers: Feast, Rower's song, Shield wall for the whole band.
More slots are earned, not bought: the first attack slot from the first kill, the second from a legend's rune, a
hird slot when you have a hird. A grown character has perhaps nine slots and a bag of forty runes, and chooses.

**Where runes come from** (the sources, each with a feel of its own):
- **Deeds.** The first rune of each craft is earned by doing: fell fifty trees and the axe rune Clean cut is
  carved for you. These are the floor every player reaches.
- **Runestones** in the world (the prop exists): read one and copy its rune. Some stand on the home island, the
  rare ones far away. The compass rule applies: the north's stones carry cold runes, the south's trading runes.
- **Beasts.** A beast slain for the first time can drop its rune (the Book of Beasts already marks the first kill):
  Boar's charge, Wolf's hunger, Bear's hide, Moose's shoulder.
- **People.** Arnor teaches a smith's rune for a favour; traders sell common ones; the first friend in the broken
  village gives the first attack rune.
- **Raids.** Castles and churches keep rare runes in their storehouses; the legendary Vikings carry the best,
  which is a reason to meet them.
- **Rune crafting**, late: two runes of a kind at a rune-cutter make a stronger one.

**Levels without a level.** Runes have a tier (common, carved, legendary) shown by the stone, and a rune in a
slot **grows with use**: an attack rune used a hundred times becomes carved, and its numbers rise. So "level up"
is per rune, from doing, and swapping a rune out costs you nothing but its growth stays on it. This keeps Robin's
"choose from the start, get everything in the end": every rune can be found, but a slot holds one.

**Exclusive by slot, not by rule.** Nothing is forbidden; the board is the limit. Taking Berserk out to put
Whirlwind in is the choice, and it can be undone at any hearth (not in a fight).

**Interface.** A third board beside the character panel and the bag: a dark leather **rune board** with the
slots as carved hollows, each hollow shaped by its kind (a sword notch, a boot, a shield), and the bag's runes
drag into them. A rune in a slot is also shown on the fight hotbar (actives in 1 to 6) or as a small glyph by the
chart (passives). Hover reads the rune: name, what it does, its tier and growth.

**Tone.** Runes are the right Norse thing: the Vikings believed runes carried power, and carving one was a deed.
Names from the futhark where they fit (Fehu for wealth and trade, Uruz the aurochs for strength, Raido the ride
for travel, Algiz the elk for guarding, Sowilo the sun for a torch's light) with a plain name beside them.

### Questions for Robin

2. How many slots to start with, and are new slots earned (recommended) or always there?
3. Should a rune in a slot grow with use, so "levelling" is per rune?
4. Do deeds (fifty trees felled) carve runes for free, so every player gets a floor?

Suggestion for that menu (Claude): **the casting cloth**. Vikings cast runes: stones carried in a leather pouch,

## Music (Robin's brief, 2026-10-05; not decided)

Robin wants original music in the style of the early MapleStory soundtrack (2003 to 2005): MIDI-based, expressive
MIDI programming, nostalgic JRPG/MMORPG character, playful melodies, accessible but slightly sophisticated harmony,
a small number of clearly defined instruments (piano, flute or clarinet, celesta or music box, strings, harp,
acoustic bass, light percussion). Free tools: LMMS or REAPER, Surge XT and Vital, Spitfire LABS and BBC SO
Discover, Polyphone with free SoundFonts for the older General MIDI character (preferred over realistic
orchestral libraries), Audacity, Valhalla Supermassive and TDR Nova. Workflow: MIDI arrangement first, then
instruments and SoundFonts, then velocity and timing, then EQ, reverb and mastering. Claude as composition and
production assistant that makes and changes MIDI files and arranges. Analysis of existing tracks (tempo, key,
chords, melody, bass, instruments, structure) as inspiration for wholly original pieces.

### Claude's thoughts

**Two routes, and a hybrid.**
- **A. The DAW route** (Robin's brief): Claude writes MIDI files (a Node script can write a .mid from note data),
  Robin opens them in LMMS or REAPER, picks SoundFonts, renders to OGG. Real mixing and reverb. But it breaks the
  project's rule that there are no asset files (a fine exception if chosen), the loop is slow (every change is an
  export, a render and a listen), and Claude cannot drive the DAW: there is no practical integration with LMMS,
  and REAPER's scripting would be a project of its own.
- **B. The in-code route**: a small sequencer in the game (Web Audio) plays songs written as note data in
  \`src/music.js\` through six to eight synthesized General-MIDI-like voices (an FM piano, a breathy flute, a
  bell for celesta and music box, a soft string pad, a plucked harp, a round acoustic bass, shaker and rim). No
  files, which keeps the rule; the early-Maple character comes from exactly this kind of plain GM voice; Claude
  writes and changes songs directly and Robin hears them in the browser within a minute; a **Song Editor** page
  (play, stop, loop, tempo, per-track instrument, volume, mute, transpose, "Settings as text") lets Robin tweak.
  The ceiling is the synthesis quality: good for the style, not as rich as a SoundFont piano.
- **Hybrid (recommended):** B is the writing room and the preview, with an **Export MIDI** button. A song that
  works can then go down route A for a SoundFont rendering if its sound is wanted, and the game can play either
  the in-code version or an OGG. Start with B; decide on A per song.

**What early Maple sounds like, in rules Claude can compose by** (from knowledge of the style, not from any
specific track): tempos 100 to 140; bright major keys and relative minors (F, Bb, C, G, D minor); eight-bar
phrases in intro, A, A', B, bridge, back to A, looping at one and a half to two and a half minutes; harmony of
I, IV, V and vi with jazz colour (ii–V–I turnarounds, secondary dominants, a sudden key change up a tone for
the B section, major sevenths and sixths on the tonic); a singable lead on flute, clarinet or piano in stepwise
lines with leaps of a sixth at phrase ends; harp or celesta arpeggios in even eighths under it; a root-and-fifth
or walking acoustic bass; light percussion (shaker, rim, soft kick) that drops out in the bridge; strings held
underneath; velocities that breathe (louder at the top of a phrase, softer at the end) and small timing pushes.

**Analysing tracks:** Claude cannot listen. Robin can take a track's tempo, key and chord progression (by ear, or
from a fan transcription or a MIDI of it) and give them to Claude; Claude then writes something original in that
frame. The analysis stays a description; nothing is copied.

**Where music goes in the game:** by place and state, with crossfades: the camp by day (warm, flute), the forest
(celesta, slower), night (sparser, strings), the sea (harp, wide), danger when a beast turns on you (percussion
in, melody out), a village (clarinet, bouncier). Six pieces to start, each looping.

## Four big problems: the year, the scale, a living world, the great towns (Robin, 2026-10-14; thoughts, not decided)

Robin asked (2026-10-14): how the islands resetting at the new year makes sense for the player; how a huge world of massive
islands and lots of sea makes sense in a 2D game, where scale is strangely hard; how the world feels alive; and whether there
should be a few massive handcrafted towns with big jarls beside many generated ones. These are my thoughts with a recommendation
each. **Nothing here is decided.** The questions for Robin are at the end.

### 1. The year turning

The reset should never touch the land, only the people. Coasts, mountains, caves and groves stay, so what the player learned
exploring stays true. What turns over is the settlement layer, and it should turn over by cause, not at random:

- **A place nobody touched grows:** a house or two more, a wall it did not have, a new jarl when the old one dies. The player
  comes back and recognises it, changed.
- **A place you sacked is rebuilt** by whoever moves in: survivors who remember you, or strangers who do not. This is the
  regenerated case, and it reads as a consequence of your own raid.
- **A place you emptied of people decays to a ruin**, and a ruin is resettled some years later (the starter island's steading is
  what that looks like).
- **A place you hold never turns over.** That is the whole reward for claiming, and the hird defends it.

The year wants a rhythm the player feels, not a counter: summer the raiding season, autumn the haul home, winter at home with
storms that make sailing foolish, spring the news. **The year ends when you winter at home**, so it is the player's choice, and
the world's loot refills with it: a church raided this year has its silver again next year. The player learns what changed
through rumour and the chart, never a popup: a trader says Steinarsheim has a stone wall now; on the chart a place seen last
year fades to a question mark until it is seen again (the map shows nothing until seen, decided 2026-10-07).

### 2. Scale in two dimensions

Why it feels hard: in 3D you see the mountain from far off; top-down you see one screen of 29 by 18 metres and nothing beyond
it, so distance can only be felt through travel time, the map and signs of things you cannot see. Three levers:

- **The sea is the huge thing, the islands are dense.** Walking stays an adventure of obstacles (decided 2026-10-09). Sailing
  is where size lives: the camera pulls out under sail so the hull is small on a big sea, crossings take minutes, and a crossing
  eats a day of the calendar. A long crossing with nothing in sight can run the clock faster until something is sighted, so a
  voyage feels long without being tedious.
- **The chart is the horizon.** In a 2D game the map is where scale is seen: it fills in as you sail, keeps your track, and shows
  nothing you have not seen. The unknown edge of the chart is what makes the world big, not the number of tiles.
- **Signs from afar:** smoke columns drawn tall enough to show over the trees before the village does, a beacon fire at night, a
  sail on the edge of the sea, a bell heard before the church is seen. These give the far-off presence 3D gets for free.

Sizes to try: lowland islands two to three times the big island, several places each, reached along the coast by boat rather
than overland (Vikings sailed; coasts are the roads), and seas of several minutes under sail. The longship already longer than
the screen and towns wider than it are the right kind of cue.

### 3. A living world

Alive means things happen without you, things remember you, and the player can see both. Cheapest first:

- **Other ships.** Three to five vessels moving between jetties on their own errands, a fishing boat off every hamlet, a patrol
  from the trading hub, now and then a rival raider's sail. Nothing makes a sea alive like something else on it.
- **A world tick once a game day.** Each place's state advances: wealth grows, a garrison recruits, a sacked town rebuilds, a
  jarl ages. News of events spreads by ship from place to place and people's lines draw on it; the legendary Vikings' names can
  travel the same way (the open question of 2026-10-04).
- **Warning beacons** along a coast that light when your sail is seen, so the next town is ready. Real Viking-age practice
  (vardar), and it makes the approach to a raid a decision: by night, from the far side.
- **Seasons you can see:** autumn colours, snow, fog, the sea state. Also how the year's turning becomes visible.
- **Wildlife with its own business:** wolves hunting deer rather than only you, birds over a carcass, seals on a skerry.

### 4. Handcrafted or generated towns

Both, with a clear split. **A few handcrafted anchors**: the trading hub and perhaps three or four great seats, each with a named
jarl, a story, a legendary Viking and a reason to go there. These persist across years and change by story state rather than
regeneration. **Everything else generated** from archetypes as the Village Editor already does, the names and the compass rules
deciding what kind of place stands where. The anchors are still placed by the generator, so their position differs each world,
with their surroundings generated and only the town itself hand-laid. The Village Editor can save a design, so a handcrafted town
is a saved layout plus a sheet of people, loot and lines: cheap to build, and the world gets memorable places without losing
freshness.

### Robin's answers (2026-10-14; these are the direction, also in CLAUDE.md)

1. **Seasons Stardew Valley style, but the player decides when a season moves**, at a moment at the end of every season. It
   must not be abusable, and the player must not feel stressed about seasons moving: how that balances is to be worked out.
   **Seasons should feel different and give access to different things and mechanics.** The whole time and season aspect is
   to be worked on later.
2. **A system of "controlled randomness".** A destroyed village or town may, after winter, be **rebuilt by survivors and be
   stronger**, be **scavenged by bandits who make it their home**, or **fade away and be overtaken by nature**. And **every
   island gets events generated after winter**: people settling and making a small village (boats by the shore and a path up
   to it); trolls moving into a new cave (the cave changes, not the layout); new creatures showing up, such as Näcken;
   destroyed villages rebuilt in various ways; villages and towns that were not raided expanding and getting bigger.
3. **The camera pulled out under sail: yes.** A faster clock: not sure; later, with the time and season work.
4. **Nothing handcrafted**: everything generated in a controlled way (so no anchors; the hub and the great seats are
   generated too, with their jarls).
5. **Warning beacons: a good idea**, kept here as an idea (not decided when they come).

Open after the answers: what the moment at the end of a season is (a feast? the first snow? sleeping at home?) and what
stops it being abused; what each season gives and takes; the list of after-winter events per island and their odds (the
controlled randomness: which outcomes a sacked place can have by what happened to it); bandits as a people (who they are,
what they hold, whether they raid); what a troll's move does to a cave; how Näcken and the other väsen arrive.

## Combat: what would make it more engaging (research, 2026-10-15; suggestions, not decided)

Robin asked to look online at how 2D action games make combat engaging and to come with suggestions, after the parry chain was
built. What the sources agree on, and what of it the game already has:

- **Readable, consistent tells** ("the fairness contract": the same look always means the same timing and the same answer; a
  colour or sound for "parry this" against "get out of the way"). We have the drawn-back clip, dust and a glint (`tell`), but
  nothing says which blows cannot be parried (the Dane axe, a troll's sweep, a heavy blow).
- **A cap on attackers at once** (DOOM's and The Last of Us Part II's attack tokens). We have it: `MEN.slots`, `gapHero`, the ways
  they come.
- **Hit-stop, small and short** (50 to 100 ms on a heavy hit). We have it, weighted by weapon.
- **A reward for reading a tell**, not only safety: Sekiro's deflect breaks posture; Windblown slows time for a dodge timed to the
  swing; TARAE refunds part of a dodge. We have the parry chain.
- **Reward pushing forward over turtling**: DOOM's glory kills give back health when you are low; Bloodborne's rally gives back
  health you lost if you hit back quickly. We have nothing of it.
- **Enemies with one clear job, mixed**: a tank that opens space, a fragile damage dealer to kill first, a support, a flanker;
  Hyper Light Drifter's packs and leaders. We have weapons and bowmen, but every man fights the same way.
- **Matchups through stagger, not damage** (Ghost of Tsushima's stances: the right answer opens a foe, the wrong one still works).
  We have some (an axe breaks a guard, a club staggers, a spear keeps a man off).

**Suggestions, in the order I would build them** (all mine; each is small on the engine we have). **1 to 4 are built (2026-10-15,
Robin: "Number 1, but that when you have reached parry x4, next attack is a finishing move. Number 2 Number 3 Number 4"; see
"Fighting men" in CLAUDE.md under the parry chain). 5 to 8 wait.**

1. **Posture: the chain's payoff.** Every parry and every blow a man stops on his shield fills his balance; full, he breaks: on his
   knees for two seconds, open to a finishing blow (a short kill with the hero untouchable, as DOOM's), or to **taking his
   weapon** (Robin's decided system), or he yields. A parry chain of four breaks him at once. So the combo ends in something, and a
   player who mostly blocks still gets there, slower.
2. **Win it back** (Bloodborne's rally, the reward rule): part of a hit you take shows as a fading stretch of the bar, and blows you
   land within three seconds win it back. Aggression after a mistake, never a punishment.
3. **The unparryable tell**: a red glint and a deeper sound on blows that cannot be parried (heavy blows, the Dane axe, a troll's
   sweep, a bear's rearing swipe); white stays "parry this". The answer to red is a dodge.
4. **A perfect dodge**: a dash that starts in the last 0.15 s before a blow lands slows time for a moment, gives back the dash's
   stamina, and leaves you beside him with his back half turned (a blow from there does more). The second skill beside the parry,
   and the answer to red.
5. **Feints from sharp men**: a man of good wits sometimes holds his windup a moment longer or stops it, to bait an early parry
   (the anti-spam rule already makes a raised guard worthless for half a second). The parry stays readable, never mashable.
6. **Men with jobs**, from their weapon and wits, with a few new behaviours: the shieldman (holds his guard, shoves; the axe opens
   him), the spearman (keeps you at the point, falls back), the berserker (no shield, fast and relentless: parry or dodge), the
   bowman (fires during the waits; kill him first), the leader (his shout steadies the rest; cut him down and the band's heart
   breaks: `bandHeart` exists). Fights are then made of mixes: a shieldman in front of a bowman, two berserkers and a leader.
7. **A shield bash**: a shove with the shield (a new key, or K tapped while stepping in) that knocks a man back; into a wall it
   stuns him, into water or fire it hurts. Uses the world we have (walls, cliffs, fires, the sea).
8. **Weapons that answer foes**: say it on the card and show it in the hit (the axe's broken guard already sounds and looks
   different); with one bar now, switching weapons mid-fight is a number key, which makes matchups a real choice.

## Base building: making it fun in 2D (Robin and Claude, 2026-10-24; not decided except where marked)

**Places (Robin, 2026-10-25; the sleeping place built, the rest waiting).** A part of the village is a *place*: you set down its post
and drag out a square, free of walls, and what stands inside gives it a level that unlocks bonuses or new mechanics. A square, not a
circle (Robin); not tied to the bed or any one object, which would limit where it goes (Robin); in and out of houses alike (Robin);
and the first place marked could be how building is first taught, through Arnor's words. Kinds to come, from the list of a Viking
village's areas: the hall, the kitchen and hearth, the storehouse (stabbur), the drying racks and smokehouse, the brewhouse, the
kitchen garden, the byre, the sheepfold, the smithy, the weaving house, the woodworking yard, the tar kiln and charcoal pit, the
tannery, the boathouse, the jetty, the treasure hall, the trophy wall, the holy grove (the rune altar), the burial ground, the thing
place, the watchtower, the training ground, the armoury, the well, the midden, the bathhouse and the guest house. Open: whether a
place's square grows with its level (my proposal) and how a second kind should read on the ground beside the first; the hird's own
building (Robin: the hirdmen get one, not beds in the sleeping place).

From the building review (`docs/building-review.md`, with the pictures in `docs/pictures/building/` and Colin, AD's view in
`C:\dev\SG\Art Director\out\building_review\VIEW.md`) and the talk with Robin after it. **Decided and built:** the world outside dims
and softens when you stand in a room (Robin's idea, the gentle look; `INS` in the game). **Liked by Robin, not built:** the cutaway
inside (the roof gone, the north wall full height, the south wall a knee-high stub, only the storey you are on).

**Why 2D building is different (Claude).** The 3D builders (Valheim, Enshrouded, Minecraft, Grounded) win on free form: shaping a
building and walking round it. From above a house is mostly its roof, so ours should win on what a building does and means. What
the good 2D builders share: everything placed does something (Stardew's sprinklers and scarecrows), rooms are for people and their
quality matters (RimWorld, Terraria's housing), what stands next to what is the game (Islanders, Dorfromantik), and the system does
the craft so anyone makes something lovely (Townscaper).

**Robin's ideas (2026-10-24):**
- **Things boost what is round them.** A well gives access to water; a bag of salt opens more recipes; building is about boosting
  parts with different things and expanding and improving your village.
- **Houses and rooms with jobs.** For example a treasure hall: the more valuables in it, the more respect, but the same silver is
  wanted at markets, so the player chooses between showing it and spending it.
- **The outside dims when you go in** (built).
- **Your hirdmen and the people of your village idle round it and do simple tasks.**

**Claude's shape for them:**
- **Stations with a reach.** Things placed within it improve the station or open new things, like Valheim's tanning rack by the
  workbench. Historical pairs: a well by the hearth opens stews and brewing (and water for a bucket line); a salt barrel opens salted
  meat and fish that keep through winter; a smokehouse by the drying racks, smoked fish; bee skeps near flowers, honey, and with a mead
  barrel by the hearth, mead for the feast; a tanning rack by the workbench, better leather gear; an anvil by the furnace, better
  metalwork; a loom by the hearth, cloth. Placing the salt barrel is holding the salt, so it keeps the rule that recipes appear from
  their key ingredient. While placing, the reach shows as a soft ring and what it touches lights up (how things look, not a label).
  It gives the seasons work too: winter is when salted and smoked food matters.
- **A room is what its contents make it** (RimWorld): a high seat and chests of valuables make a treasure hall; beds a sleeping hall;
  a forge a smithy; stalls and hay a byre (on the end of the house it warms it, as the animals did); a storehouse, a brewhouse, a
  boathouse, a bathhouse. Its name shows quietly the first time you step in.
- **Treasure and trophies.** Treasure (silver, rings, church plate) can be shown in the hall or spent; trophies (the jarl's shield, a
  raided hall's carved post, a famous enemy's weapon) cannot be sold and only add respect, each with its story. What respect buys:
  better men willing to join, the jarl's notice (the story onward), better prices and welcome at markets, the villagers' words (the Lore
  Director's). Not a hall that draws raiders for now: defending your home is a later chapter, and as a punishment it fights Robin's rule.
- **The hall tells your saga.** What raids bring home goes on display; a carved post for each great raid; a runestone with his name for
  each fallen hirdman; people speak of what they see.
- **You plan a steading and it grows.** You mark the ground (the hall here, the byre, the smithy by the well, the boathouse at the
  shore) and the hird raises it over days and seasons by the villages' own grammar (`src/village.js`), in their look; every wall and
  bench can still be placed by hand. Robin did not answer yet whether this feels like building to him.
- **The hird's homes.** Each man wants a bed, then a place of his own, then a better one; a good hall draws better men.
- **The hird and the people live in your steading.** Give it the villages' routines (spots, a round, sitting at the fire, sleeping,
  watching you), the spots taken from what you have built: the fire pit to sit at in the evening, the beds to sleep in, the table to eat
  at dawn, the well to fetch water from. Tasks you can see, their animations already the villagers': a woodcutter splitting logs at the
  chopping block and stacking the woodpile, water carried from the well to the hearth, a cook at the pot, a fisher at the end of the
  jetty, a smith at the anvil. What you place decides what your men do. Today the hird's jobs (`Hird.DEF.jobs`: crafter, woodcutter,
  cook, keeper) are numbers ticking over while a man stands at his place.

## Playing parts of the game on the phone (Robin, 2026-10-27; ideas, not decided)

Robin: the game is played on the PC, but certain parts could be played from the phone while away, on the train to work.

Clive, GD's view (suggestions):
- **The rule that keeps it healthy:** the phone gives something small, or a plan, never power the PC game needs. Nothing on
  the phone runs out, rots or is missed while you are away (Robin's "reward, don't punish"), so nobody feels they must open it.
- **Good fits, best first:**
  1. **Send the hird out** (expeditions): pick fellows and an errand (fish the sound, trade at Old Aslak's village, scout a
     water, hunt), they are gone for real hours, and they come back with goods, a wound, a rumour, a story told in Alistair,
     LD's lines. It feeds the hird and the rumours, which have no way to surface yet.
  2. **Fishing**: one self-contained round already (`src/fishing.js`, no drawing, no browser globals); the steering (W reels,
     A and D turn its head) maps to a thumb on the water. The catch waits in a creel at home.
  3. **Planning the home**: lay out houses, places and fences on a plan of your ground; the PC shows it as ghosts to build.
  4. **The book and the chart**: read the Lore Book and the quests, name places, set marks, plan a course.
  5. **The ship's look** and its name, sail and shields.
- **Not on the phone:** fighting, raids, the rite, gathering: the game's heart stays where its controls are.
- **What it takes:** the save is in the PC browser's storage (`file://`), so the phone cannot see it. A shared save means a
  small server and a sign-in (the same thing `docs/release-and-coop.md` needs for playing with friends), or for a first trial a
  phone page that keeps its own little save and hands its results to the PC as a code. The phone writes orders and results,
  never the whole save: the PC applies them when it loads, so the two never fight over one save.
- **A first trial, cheap:** the fishing round as a phone page on the game's own module and numbers, to feel whether it is fun
  on a thumb before anything is synced.

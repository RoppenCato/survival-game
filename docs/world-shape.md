# The shape of the world: raids, exploration, special islands, claims and the year

**A proposal (2026-10-19); Robin finds the idea good and has answered the first questions (below).** Robin set out the directional challenges below; the problems list and the
solutions are mine. Nothing here is decided or built. Where it touches decided things (`CLAUDE.md`: the compass, the
chapters, the year, the hird, the raid) it keeps them and says how they fit.

### Worked out from round two (mine, waiting)

**Opening the raids at home.** Before your first raid abroad, home's villages are not raid places at all: no stores behind
doors, no raid state, and the jarl's peace holds. Your first raid in the second circle is the turn: word of it comes home
with the traders, and the jarl calls you to his hall and asks for a share. Pay, and the peace holds a while longer; refuse,
or come back with enough men, and his waters are open to you: his villages keep stores now (his tax), his men answer you.

**Overthrowing the jarl.** Raiding his villages weakens him (fewer men at his hall, less in his stores) and wins you
loot and men. When you are ready, you raid his hall: his hird at the doors, the jarl himself a man with a good weapon
and his best men, a set piece of the raid we have, not a boss type. Take his high seat and home water is yours to claim.

**Claiming home, one island at a time.** After the jarl falls, each village can be claimed: a claim stone and a hirdman to
keep it, as decided. Two ways, by what you did before: villages you spared and traded with swear to you willingly; ones you
raided you claim by force, and they give less. A claimed village sends food, wool and recruits and works for you, so home
water becomes your realm and the big base Robin wants grows across islands.

**Circles, without looking like circles.** The circles stay (control: the further out, the harder and the newer the
metal), but nobody should see a ring:
- A circle is a count of crossings from home, not a radius. Waters are scattered by noise, so the map is lumpy: one
  second-circle water lies close, another far; a third-circle water may sit behind a second-circle one, reachable only
  through it.
- Circles are not complete: the second circle has four seas, the third five to seven, some dead ends, one behind sea
  ice that opens only in summer.
- The next metal is teased before its circle: a trader selling one bar, a wreck with a broken blade, a fallen star with
  a lump of strange iron. You know it exists and roughly where.
- Each circle's metal comes in a different way in each sea (bog iron, ore, salvage, trade), so the same step feels
  different by direction.
- The metals, in order (mine; the names of the later ones are the Lore Director's): flint, copper, iron, steel, then
  something legendary at the edge. The ring metals (bronze, silver, gold, Odin's ring) run beside them as the world's
  big rewards.

This matches what was decided already: distance from home sets how hard a place is, direction what kind.

## Worked out (mine, 2026-10-19; waiting for Robin)

### The rings

- **Ring 0, home water**: the starter island, the big island with its mountain and the troll, and many more islands: a
  few medium (150 to 250 tiles), many small (30 to 80), skerries. Villages only (farmsteads, fishing hamlets, a trading
  post), friendly, and the simple jarl's hall on one medium island. Flint, then copper (on more than one island, so it is
  never a single place). What you do: settle, build, trade, hear rumours, recruit your first hirdmen, help and be helped.
  Chapters 1 and 2.
- **Ring 1, the four seas** (north, south, east, west): the same level. Raiding starts here. Every sea has the ring's
  tier material (iron) in its own form, and things only it has. Chapter 3 is the first of them (the south: the lowlands are
  built), then the others.
- **Ring 2 and beyond**: harder, further, the next ring metal; the far west (Kvennaey, as its lore says "far west") lies
  out here.

### What ties you to home at first: the ship, not a wall

The edge of home water is open sea: the swell grows and a raft or a small boat cannot cross it (turned back by the swell,
never punished: it drifts home). A sea-going ship can: a hull from the big shipwright's bench, copper rivets, a sail woven
from the villages' wool, tar from pine, and a crew of hirdmen to row. Getting all of that is most of chapters 1 and 2, and
it is earned, not a key. The simple jarl can be the one who says the seas are open to you (a feast, a gift of a sail),
so the moment is a story beat, not a menu.

### The four seas: the same level, different things

Each has the tier material in its own form (so any direction moves you on), its own look, its own places to raid, and
side things that fill the necklace, ring and trinket slots, the bag and the base, never required:

| Sea | Look | Iron, its way | Raids | Only here (optional) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| North | birch, fell, snow, long winters | ore in the mountains | fell farms, a jarl's hall | furs, walrus ivory, whale oil (a longer torch), skis, reindeer |
| South | chalk, fields, orchards, mild | bought or looted bars, a town's bloomery | churches, towns (silver) | bread and wheat, dyes for sails and clothes, glass beads, honey |
| East | marsh, reeds, rivers | bog iron and charcoal | river forts, rich traders' boats | amber (necklaces), the trading hub, foreign goods |
| West | heath, sea cliffs, wind | salvaged from wrecks | island monasteries, wreckers | seal skin, puffin eggs, the sunstone (finds the sun in fog), the way further west |

Each sea also has its own beasts, its own recruits (a northern hunter with a bow, a southern smith) and its own named slot.

### Islands: a recipe, rolled with guarantees

A sea is rolled first: ring, direction, climate, its specialty, its kinds of island, its named slot. Then each island is a
recipe with its own seed (the world's seed, the sea, its number), so it can be rebuilt from the seed and saved as what
changed:

1. **Size and shape**: round, long, a ring round a bay, a steep rock.
2. **Relief**: flat, hills, or a mountain (the mountain code we have).
3. **Climate**: the sea's, varied a little per island and blended where seas meet.
4. **Kind**: settled, wild, mountain, marsh, holy, ruin, fishing; settlements by kind (the Village archetypes).
5. **Features** from a pool weighted by kind and sea: caves, waterfalls, lookouts, groves, bogs, rune stones, ship
   settings, ruins.
6. **Beasts** by climate; **resources** by the sea's specialty.
7. **Good build spots**, scored: shelter, flat ground, water, timber near, a view, a landing.

Controlled, not loose: every sea guarantees its specialty on at least two islands and its iron on at least one, every big
island at least one good build spot and a landing, home water copper on two islands and a village within reach of the
start; the rest is rolled.

### Biomes become climates, and seasons are a layer on top

A **climate** is a table of looks: ground colours, trees, rocks, grass, flowers, light, weather, beasts (home meadow,
birch fell, chalk field, marsh, heath). What sits on an island is its kind; how hard is its ring. The lowland "biome" in
the code becomes the chalk climate plus the town kind.

**Seasons are a second axis: the look is climate times season.** Every climate entry gets four palettes from the start
(spring, summer, autumn, winter), even while only summer is drawn: blossom, full green, autumn colours, snow or rain.
Seasons change paint and props, never the land: the ground is baked again when the season turns, trees swap to their
season's sprite, the grass changes colour. Climates differ in their year (the north's snow comes in autumn and the sea ices
over in winter, so you sail north in summer; the south has a mild wet winter; the west has autumn storms). That gives the
year its rhythm without a rule saying so, and fits the invited season (summer) for Kvennaey.

## Robin's challenges (2026-10-19, in his words, shortened)

Raids and exploration are the two big things. You build a big base where your community grows. You travel to special
islands (being written in the lore): you hear of one and go, or stumble on one. Maybe biomes should be scrapped as
progression and kept only as looks (grassy, snowy, rocky). Lots of random elements, so a new playthrough feels new and you
want to explore and to find nice places to build. Raids need islands to raid, which fights with special islands being rare:
spread out a lot, or a massive world that swaps a generic island for a special one when you first hear of it (messy). You
expand to other islands. Raided islands rebuild and get new things if you do not claim them (the seasonal idea); not sure how
mechanically. Raids as instances would solve some of it but defeats the big generated world.

## The problems, as I see them

1. **Density against rarity.** Raiding wants many targets within a short sail. Special islands want to be rare and far. In one
   uniform sea you get either a crowded sea where the special is next door, or an empty one where every raid is a voyage.
2. **Authored islands in a generated world.** The lore's islands are written; the sea is rolled. Where do they go, and what
   does "hearing of one" do if it already exists? Swapping a generic island for a special one at hear-time only works where
   the player has not yet sailed, and it breaks the chart's promise that what was seen stays true.
3. **Biomes do three jobs at once.** Today a biome is a look, a set of contents (trees, beasts, settlements) and a difficulty
   rung. Robin wants the look kept and the gate dropped, but something must still make the far places harder.
4. **A raided place has no life after the raid.** The raid is built; what the ruin becomes is not. Without a lifecycle the
   world empties as you play it, and a raided village is a dead object on the map forever.
5. **The clock.** Robin's year is player-driven (you end a season). What turns the world: the year only, or each season? What
   stops a player from never ending the season, and what stops farming one village? Changes must make sense and feel earned.
6. **Instance or world.** An instanced raid gives control (a fresh target every time) but kills persistence, claiming and the
   "I burnt that place" feeling. The world way needs 4 and 5 solved.
7. **Claiming against rebuilding.** If a raided island rebuilds, what happens when you claim it instead? What does a second
   island of yours do for you, and what threatens it without random raids?
8. **Exploration needs a reason and a way to know where to go.** A chart that shows nothing until seen is right, but sailing
   blind into an empty sea is not fun. Finding build spots, raid targets and special islands all need a knowledge layer.
9. **Novelty inside a playthrough and between playthroughs.** Rolling a new sea gives the second; something must give the
   first (the world changing under you), or exploration ends once the sea is charted.
10. **Travel.** Islands far enough apart to feel like a voyage are far enough apart to be boring. Distance must be short where
    you go often and long where you go once.

Most of these are one problem seen from different sides: **the world needs a structure that is dense near and sparse far,
and a clock that renews the near part.** The rest follows.

## Solutions

### 1 and 10. Waters, not one sea

The sea is made of **waters** (a Norse *sund* or *fjord* or *haf*: a named body of water with its islands round it). Within a
water, islands are near: a few minutes' sail, the raiding range. Between waters is a crossing: long, open, with nothing to
see but the next water's first headland. Home waters hold the starter island and the big island (the two we spawn now).

Each water has: 3 to 8 generic islands (settled, wild, mountain, marsh, as rolled), **one slot for a named island** (a lore
island, or empty), one or two "nothing" islets (skerries, a seal rock) so not every island is a place. The compass sets the
kind of water (north cold, south settled, east rivers and trade, west open and unknown), the water's index from home sets
how hard its people are. This is the two-island world we have, repeated by the compass.

What it buys: raid targets are always a short sail from wherever you anchor; special islands are one per water, so rare by
construction; a crossing feels like exploring because it is one; and "distance from home sets how hard" becomes a count of
crossings, which the player feels.

### 2. Named islands take a slot, rolled into place, never swapped

The lore's islands are placed at world generation into the named slot of the water their lore says (Kvennaey in the far
western water, the trading hub in an eastern water, a great seat in the south), with their exact position rolled inside that
water. The chart hides them like everything else until seen. **A rumour marks the chart; it does not create the island.**

So both of Robin's ways work: you hear of Kvennaey from a daughter met elsewhere and sail west to the mark, or you cross west
with no word and find a beach of wrecks. Nothing is swapped, the chart never lies, and we never need the "massive world".
Not every named slot is filled in every playthrough: a sea of six waters might hold four lore islands, drawn from the pool, so
two playthroughs differ in which islands exist, not only where.

The "generic island becomes special" trick stays useful in one small form: **events after winter** (decided already) may put a
*new thing* on an island you have not claimed (a new settlement, trolls in a cave, a jarl's men). That is late binding of
content, not of islands, and it is allowed only where the player has not stood since the year turned.

### 3. Split the biome into three layers

- **Climate** is a look by latitude and compass: lush green (home), birch and fell and snow (north), pine and rock, chalk and
  field and orchard (south), marsh and reed (east). Climate blends across a big island. It decides trees, ground, weather,
  light. It is what Robin means by "templates for different looking places".
- **Kind** is what sits on an island: a settled island (villages, a church, a hall), a wild one (beasts, groves, a troll's
  mountain), a marsh (bog iron), a fishing island, a holy island, a ruin. Rolled per island from the water's kind.
- **Rung** is the water's distance from home: it scales the men (numbers, arms, walls), the loot and the beasts. The lowland
  "biome" we built becomes "the southern climate" plus "the town kind" plus "rung 2".

No gates: you may sail anywhere. You struggle at rung 3 with flint; that is the whole gate, as decided. The chapters map
onto this without change: chapter 1 and 2 are home waters, chapter 3 the first crossing (south, the first raid), chapter 4
on the next waters by the compass.

### 4 and 5. A raided place lives by the seasons; the season turn is the world's turn

A settlement is in one **state**, and changes state only at a season turn (Robin's "the player decides when a season moves"):

| State | What it is | After the next turn, if unclaimed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Thriving** | Whole, people at their routines, loot behind doors | Stays; grows a little (more loot, a new building) every year left alone |
| **Raided** | What you left: dead, fled, doors broken, maybe burnt | One of: *rebuilt*, *taken*, *overgrown* (rolled by how badly it was hit and who survived) |
| **Rebuilt** | Survivors back, poorer, with a palisade and a watch (harder, less loot) | Thriving again after another turn, stronger than before, up to a cap |
| **Taken** | Bandits or another jarl's men hold it: no priests, no traders, armed men, loot they took | Stays taken until raided; may send men against your claims (see 7) |
| **Overgrown** | A ruin with nature over it; a build spot, a place for a new settlement later | After a year, a new settlement may land (the controlled randomness), or it stays a ruin |
| **Claimed** | Yours (a claim stone stands) | Never changes by itself; your hird mends it (mending is built) |

Why it feels right: raiding is harvesting. A place you hit comes back changed, and the change is a consequence (people
remember; the third raid on one village finds a garrison or nobody), so farming one village pays less and costs more each
time, while leaving one alone makes it fat. The world never empties: at the turn the near waters renew.

Why the player ends a season: within a season the near targets are used up and nothing rebuilds, which pushes you outward
(the next water) or homeward (building, training, the hird). Ending the season brings the renewal, the new events, the next
season's things (Robin: seasons open different things; summer is the invited season for Kvennaey). A player who never ends
the season is not punished; he simply runs out of near things to do, which is the nudge. **No abuse:** a raided place is
empty until it changes state, and a rebuilt one is stronger; there is nothing to farm.

Should every season turn roll the world, or only winter's? **Every turn, a step; winter's, the big roll.** A raided village
becomes rebuilt or taken at the next turn (one step); new settlements, new caves, trolls, new creatures arrive only after
winter (Robin's decision stands). Four small changes a year and one big one keeps the near world alive without making it
unrecognisable.

### 6. No instances. The raid is a mode in the world

The raid stays what is built: your ship on their shore, your men behind you, the place waking to the noise. What makes it
feel like "a raid" rather than "a walk" is already there (the hird follows only on raids; a drawn blade; the gates barred to
armed men). Persistence is the reward: the ruin stays on the chart with your mark on it, and the lifecycle above gives it a
future. Instancing would give nothing the waters do not.

### 7. Claims: expansion is a decision about people, and threats are answers

A claim stone on another island makes it yours and stops its lifecycle. An **outpost** needs hirdmen as keepers (decided:
every stone after the first), so expansion is spending people, and the hird's growth is what lets you spread. An outpost gives
what its island has (timber, fish, bog iron, a quarry, a safe anchorage one water out) and holds what you bring there; later a
hirdman-crewed boat can carry it home.

Threats to claims are **answers, never random** (idea bank 4): a *taken* place in the same water, or a jarl whose hall you
raided, may come for your outpost at a season turn. It is always announced first as a rumour (a lead: "men gather at X to
come for your stone at Y") so you can garrison, strike first, or let it go. The home claim is rarely tested; outposts are.
Losing one costs goods and the stone, not hours of rebuilding: your men mend it.

### 8 and 9. Rumours are the knowledge layer; the chart is where they land

The rumours just made canon (whisper, lead, truth) are the thread that ties the three big things together:

- A **whisper** marks a direction or a water on the chart ("far west, an island of women").
- A **lead** marks a place ("the church at X keeps silver", "a bay in the east where the timber comes to the water", "the
  old man in Y knows the troll's cave").
- A **truth** is told only after you have seen the thing, and changes what you can do there.

Rumours are heard from the people you do not raid: trading villages, the hub, a priest, your own hird, the fisherman on a
skerry. That gives a reason to sail to places and *not* draw the blade, which is Robin's reputation brake made into play.
Build spots are rumoured too: the generator marks a few **good spots** on every island on purpose (a sheltered bay with a
flat shelf, a river mouth, a hilltop clearing, a cove under a cliff, a waterfall's foot) so that exploring finds them, the
lookout marks them, and a lead can point at one. Nice to look at and convenient are both rolled: the generator scores a spot
for shelter, flat ground, water, timber near, a view.

Novelty inside one playthrough comes from the turns (the near world changes) and from the chart filling in; between
playthroughs from the roll (which waters, which lore islands, which kinds).

## How it plays, as a year

- **Spring:** home. Build, mend, train, plant the stones; raid the near water's fat villages (rebuilt over winter, so
  changed). Rumours from the first trader's boat tell what the winter did.
- **Summer:** the voyage. Seas calm, the invited season; cross to a new water, chart it, find its named island or its best
  bay, raid its church, hear its rumours, bring back a recruit. Near targets are used up, so going far is the thing to do.
- **Autumn:** home with what you took; the outposts; harvest; the last near raids on what rebuilt; a reprisal announced.
- **Winter:** the big roll. The long nights are for the home (rested and comfort buffs, crafting, the ring); nobody attacks;
  the world renews under the ice. The year turns on your word, with a feast and the year's tally (idea bank 1).

The two big things feed each other: raids pay for the base and the ships; the ships reach the next water; the next water
has the rumour of the special island; the special island gives the rune, the hirdman, the ring metal; the hird holds the
claims; the claims make the base big. Nothing is a gate; everything is a reason.

## What I would build first (a ladder, each rung playable)

1. **The lifecycle on the big island.** Villages there get a state and a turn; a Season board ("End the summer") on the
   Escape menu for now. Raid one, end the season, see it rebuilt with a palisade. This is the smallest version of the whole
   idea and tests whether it feels right. Mostly `Village` and `Ruin` and a state in the save.
2. **A second water.** The Sea Editor lays out home waters plus one crossing to a southern water with the lowland island in
   it (the lowlands are built, not spawned). This is chapter 3's world and the first real raid.
3. **Rumours on the chart.** Whispers and leads from villagers mark the chart; the lookout marks good spots. Build spots
   scored by the generator.
4. **A claim on another island, and one answered threat.**
5. **The named slot** and the first lore island placed in it (the trading hub, as it is nearer in the lore than Kvennaey).
